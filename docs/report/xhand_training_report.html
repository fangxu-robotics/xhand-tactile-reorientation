Teaching a 12-DoF hand to flip a sponge

Reinforcement learning for in-hand reorientation with the XHand1 dexterous hand: from a vendor URDF to a policy that rights and flips a kitchen sponge in 4096 parallel simulated hands, then runs unchanged on a Franka-mounted hand for demonstration.

1. The task

A kitchen sponge (10 × 6.5 × 3.2 cm, 30 g) with a green scrub face and a yellow foam face sits in an upturned palm. Each episode names one face. The hand must turn the sponge until the named face points down and keep it there, grasped, for two continuous seconds. When the named face starts up, that is a full 180° flip about the sponge's long axis; when it starts down, the job is just to keep hold of it.

Success is measured strictly: the requested face within 20° of straight down, and at least one contact sensor touching the sponge, for 60 consecutive control steps. Letting the sponge get more than 25 cm from the palm ends the episode as a drop.

2. Curriculum

Training runs in four stages, each resumed from the previous checkpoint. The first two only ask the hand to keep and right a sponge that starts nearly in place. Stage 3 introduces the full problem: either face may be requested and the sponge spawns at any roll about its long axis. Stage 4 widens object randomization to the final ranges.

3. Training results

Three Stage 3 attempts are compared. The earlier chain used a sponge placed with its short axis along the thumb and plateaued near 23 % success with more than half of episodes dropped. Re-orienting the sponge so its long axis runs along the extended thumb, with the four fingers wrapping the long side, changed the picture: both new runs cross 60 % hold success within 1000 iterations and cut drops below 15 %. Run B starts from the Stage 2 checkpoint; Run A starts from random weights and reaches the same level about 700 iterations sooner in wall-clock terms, since it skips Stages 1 and 2. Stage 4 then continues Run B for 1500 iterations with mass, friction, actuator gains, control delay and initial-grasp offsets at their widest ranges, and keeps the same success and drop rates.

4. What changed, and what it did

Most of the progress came from fixing the environment, not tuning PPO. Each entry below is a defect found by probing the simulation directly, the fix, and its measured effect.

ENVIRONMENT · 2026-09-08

Hand was hanging fingers-down

The asset's identity rotation put the palm sideways and the fingers pointing at the floor, so the "grasp" was a hook. Probing body poses gave the base-frame axes; a fixed quaternion now maps fingers forward and palm up.

Sponge rests in the palm: 1 cm settle over 2 s, palm contact 0.285 N = the sponge's weight.

TERMINATION · 2026-09-08

Drop threshold fired on a resting sponge

"Dropped" meant more than 15 cm from the hand base, but the resting sponge already sat 10 cm away. Raised to 25 cm.

Stage 1 went from 100 % drops and 2.6-step episodes to 85 % hold success in 300 iterations (Fig. 5).

OBSERVATION · 2026-09-08

Palm contact read 20 kN

The vendor collision meshes embed the finger knuckles inside the palm hull, so self-collision on the fixed base produced enormous forces in the unfiltered contact reading. The observation now reads the sponge-filtered force matrix and self-collision is disabled.

Contact observation on the correct 0–15 N scale.

REWARD · 2026-09-08

Policy learned to throw the sponge

The alignment reward was a raw cosine in [−1, 1]. When the requested face started up, the policy earned about −1 per step for 240 steps versus a one-time −50 for dropping, so it dropped on purpose: a green-only test gave 0 successes in 363 episodes. The reward is now (1 + cos)/2 and the angular-velocity penalty was cut five-fold.

Drops on flip requests stopped being the optimal strategy.

CURRICULUM · 2026-09-08

Sponge never spawned mid-flip

Spawn tilt was ±0.2 rad, so the policy never observed intermediate orientations it would have to pass through. Spawn roll is now uniform over ±180°, matching Isaac Lab's Allegro reference.

Success climbed from 15 % to 22 %, still short of a real flip.

GRASP GEOMETRY · 2026-09-08 19:20

Long axis along the thumb

The sponge was rotated 90° so the four fingers wrap its long side and the flip becomes a roll about that axis; the thumb default pose was opened up to make room. Episode length grew from 8 s to 12 s.

Both Stage 3 runs reach 60–70 % hold success with under 15 % drops (Fig. 4). This is the current configuration.

INFRASTRUCTURE · 2026-09-08 19:43

Both runs killed by the memory watchdog

Two 4096-environment simulators in one terminal reached 23 GB on a 32 GB machine with no swap. systemd-oomd killed the desktop session. Runs were resumed sequentially from their last checkpoints as detached services, with swap added.

One simulator alone steps at 126k env-steps/s; two sharing the GPU got 51k each, so sequential costs nothing (Fig. 6).

CURRICULUM · 2026-09-08 22:52

Stage 4 under full randomization

Run B's checkpoint continued for 1500 iterations with the widest mass, friction, gain, delay and grasp-offset ranges.

70 % hold success, 9 % drops: no loss against Stage 3. Demo videos: 49 of 50 flips on the hand grid, 10 of 10 on the arm at the close and top cameras.

HARDWARE PREP · 2026-09-09

Open-loop trajectory export

For a first real-hand demo without perception, the commanded finger targets of successful simulated flips were exported and replayed open-loop on 64 freshly randomized sponges. The most replayable of 12 candidate episodes was kept. Rate-limiting the targets or replaying measured joint angles instead of commands fails: the over-shooting targets are the squeeze.

Stage 4 policy: 48 % open-loop success in sim (Stage 3 policy: 34–38 %). Packaged with a numpy-only player for the robot PC.

5. Demonstrations

Videos were recorded by replaying checkpoints in a play environment with a two-tone sponge and camera presets. The Stage 2 policy, which rights a tilted sponge, transfers to the Franka-mounted hand without retraining. With the Run B policy every flip request in the recorded videos succeeded on the fixed-wrist hand (35 of 35) and on the Franka-mounted hand (12 of 12 at the close and top cameras). Run A holds on just as well but works more slowly: on the arm it completed only 2 episodes in 20 s.

7. Next

A first hardware attempt with the exported open-loop trajectory: verify the SDK joint mapping in free air, hold the arm palm-up, place the sponge, replay. Then on the new sponge pose. Then the Phase 2 and 3 work that this environment was built for: train the proprioception-plus-contact student against the privileged teacher, map the XHand1's fingertip tactile arrays into the contact channel, and prepare the real-robot interface for the left hand in the lab.
